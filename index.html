There is a particular kind of house that looks as though it has always belonged to its hillside. It does not shout for attention, and it does not apologise for its presence either. It sits in conversation with the slope, the trees and the light.

The upcoming residence of Mr. Mandyal at Purani Mandi, designed by TSI Architects and Engineers, aims to be this kind of house. From the design visualisations, it is a home built for the long evenings of the Himalayan foothills, when the sun drops behind the ridgelines and the whole valley turns amber.

This article looks at the design closely: its composition, its materials, its balconies and terraces, its landscape, and the way of living it promises.

A setting that shapes the design

Purani Mandi sits within the Himachal Pradesh hills, a region with layered ridgelines, deodar and pine forests, and a strong tradition of stone, timber and slate in its buildings. Any new house here inherits two obligations. It must respect the landscape, and it must make good use of the views the landscape offers.

The visualisations show that TSI has taken both seriously. The house is set among tall, slender conifers and flowering shrubs, with layered mountain silhouettes behind it. The golden-hour rendering shows the sun low on the horizon and the sky washed in peach and apricot. The image also suggests the design intent: the house is oriented to catch this light, and most of its principal rooms and outdoor spaces open toward the view.

Sloping sites are demanding. They ask the architect to deal with level changes, drainage, retaining walls and the relationship between house and ground. The images show that the site has been handled with care. Low, balustraded garden walls, a paved approach and terraced planting hold the land in place while adding to the composition.

Two volumes, one idea

The most striking feature of the design is its massing. The house reads as two distinct but harmonious volumes joined together.

The first is a tall, vertical brick tower, set to one side and crowned with a classical cornice. It anchors the composition and gives the house a strong vertical accent against the horizontal sweep of the hills. The second is a broader, lighter cream-coloured block with generous glazing and layered balconies. It carries most of the living and sleeping accommodation.

The contrast works well. The brick tower is earthy, solid and slightly formal, while the stone-coloured mass is calm and open. Together they avoid the monotony of a single box, and they give the house the character of a home that has grown and been refined over time, even though it is new.

The tower also does practical work. In a hillside home, a vertical element commonly houses the staircase and circulation. It also gives the elevation depth, so the house does not read as a flat wall from the approach. The tall window on its upper level, visible in the angled view, floods the stair with daylight, which is a small luxury in a house of this scale.

Brick, stone and black steel

A house's material palette is its personality, and this one has a clear, confident voice built from three main ingredients.

  • Red brick

    The tower is clad in warm, terracotta-toned brick laid in a classic bond. Brick brings texture, colour and permanence, and its hue picks up the tones of the sunset, so at golden hour the tower seems to glow. Its use here connects the house to a familiar tradition of Indian residential architecture while keeping the look contemporary.

  • Cream stone finish

    The main volume is finished in a soft, pale, limestone-like cladding. Light stone suits the hills: it reflects the changing sky, looks luminous at dusk, and stays quiet enough to let the greenery and the brick do the talking. It also suggests thermal mass and durability, both valuable in cold winters and monsoon rains.

  • Black steel glazing

    The windows and doors sit in slim, dark, multi-paned frames. The fine grid of glazing bars adds rhythm and scale to the façade, and the dark lines give crisp definition against pale stone. At dusk, with interior lights glowing behind them, the windows turn into lanterns.

Around these three materials sit supporting elements: pale balustrades, warm timber accents at the balcony returns, dark entrance doors, and a paved approach with green joints where grass grows between the stones. The palette is restrained, and the restraint gives it strength.

Living outside as well as inside

If one idea defines this residence, it is the belief that a mountain home should be lived in outdoors as much as indoors. The design makes that belief tangible on every level.

At the entrance level, a generous porch sits beneath a balcony, framed by classical columns. Above it, that balcony is enclosed by a turned balustrade and works as an outdoor room for the floor above. In the renderings, a figure stands there with a cup in hand, looking over the valley. The image is small but tells the story: this is a house for slow mornings.

On the main façade, each floor has its own projecting balcony, and each floor's balcony is a little different. The middle level carries a deep, balustraded terrace with timber-lined reveals, giving shade and shelter. The lower level has a broad, low balcony that reads almost as a garden terrace, connecting the interior to the lawn. These layered projections cast shadows, add depth and break the façade into human-scaled pieces.

The balustrades deserve a mention. The turned, classical balusters recur across all levels, giving the house a consistent architectural language. They belong to a traditional vocabulary, yet in pale stone against black glazing and red brick they feel fresh rather than nostalgic.

The rooftop pergola

At the very top, a timber pergola sits on the roof terrace, with climbing greenery beginning to wrap around its beams. It is one of the most attractive features of the design.

Roof terraces are especially valuable in hill homes. They offer the widest views, the best sunsets and, on clear nights, an uninterrupted sky. The pergola brings just enough structure and shade to make the space usable through the day, without closing it off from the air. A surrounding balustrade keeps it safe and visually ties it to the balconies below.

One can easily imagine how such a space would be used: family gatherings on winter afternoons, tea in the early morning, festival evenings with lights strung across the beams, or simply a quiet place to watch the light change over the ridges. The pergola gives the house a fitting crown, and it echoes the timber accents seen elsewhere on the building.

Arrival and landscape

Good architecture cares about how you arrive. In these visualisations, the approach is a small pleasure in itself.

A patterned paved path winds across the garden, its joints softened by grass. It leads to a raised entrance porch with wide steps, where columns frame the dark front door. The scene in the images shows a father and child at the threshold, a moment that conveys the house's purpose better than any technical description could.

The planting supports the architecture. Tall, narrow evergreens punctuate the site vertically and echo the hills' conifers. Soft masses of white hydrangeas and lavender-toned shrubs supply colour and fragrance at ground level. A flowering tree in bloom frames one side of the composition. The lawn and the clipped hedges near the retaining wall bring a formal, English-garden order that suits the classical detailing of the house.

Wall-mounted lanterns beside the doors and windows add warmth after dark, and a tall curved lamp near the tower gives the entrance a welcoming glow. Lighting at this level is not decoration alone. On a hillside with uneven ground, it is also part of safety and wayfinding.

Light, glass and the interior

The visualisations show interiors only as glimpses through the windows, but the glimpses are telling. Lamps glow beside seating. Curtains hang in generous folds. Potted plants stand close to the glass. Framed artwork is visible on the walls. Together they suggest interiors that are warm, layered and comfortable rather than stark.

The large multi-pane windows are set in tall, floor-to-ceiling proportions on the main floors. This does several things at once. It brings daylight deep into the rooms, it frames the outdoor scenery like a series of pictures, and it makes the boundary between indoors and outdoors feel soft. Where the windows meet balcony doors, a room can open fully to the terrace, so that a living area on a fine day extends outward.

The rich, dark timber accents visible at the balcony edges hint that timber may appear indoors too, perhaps in flooring, ceilings or joinery. In a cold-climate home, timber brings warmth in every sense.

Designing for the climate

Himachal's climate asks a lot of a building: cool to cold winters, heavy monsoon rain, strong sun at altitude and a real need for durable materials. Without seeing the full drawings, one can still read several sensible responses in the design's visible features.

  • Deep overhangs and covered balconies protect walls and openings from rain while providing shaded outdoor space in summer.
  • Solid masonry walls in brick and stone offer thermal mass, helping moderate temperature swings.
  • Large glazed openings invite winter sun and daylight, reducing the need for artificial light during the day.
  • Terraced, well-drained landscape elements help the site cope with seasonal water flow.
  • Robust, low-maintenance finishes such as brick and stone weather gracefully rather than deteriorating.

These are the design's visible intentions. The final performance will depend on details that only the project's drawings and specifications can confirm, such as insulation, glazing specification, waterproofing and structural design. Still, the overall approach follows sound principles for hill architecture.

Classical ideas, contemporary spirit

It is worth pausing on the design's overall style, because it makes an interesting statement. The house borrows from classical architecture: columns, cornices, turned balustrades, symmetrical window groupings. It also borrows from contemporary architecture: large, slim-framed glazing, clean planes, cantilevered balconies and a restrained colour palette.

Not a pastiche of heavy ornament, and not a cold box that ignores its setting.

The combination avoids two common pitfalls. Instead, it feels like a house with good manners and quiet confidence. The classical details give it dignity and familiarity. The modern details give it clarity and lightness.

For a family home, this balance matters. A house should feel like a place of continuity, somewhere memories accumulate, rather than a showpiece dated by its own fashion. Timeless proportions, natural materials and simple geometry are the surest route to a house that ages well.

The role of TSI Architects and Engineers

The design's quality lies in its coherence. Every element, from the brick tower to the pergola, seems to belong to a single idea. Achieving that takes more than a good sketch. It requires close coordination between architecture and engineering: cantilevered balconies must be structurally sound, large glazed openings must be properly supported, a hillside site must be stabilised, and rooftop loads must be accounted for.

TSI Architects and Engineers bring both disciplines under one roof, and the benefit of that integration shows in a design that is ambitious yet buildable. The visual language stays consistent because the people drawing the details also understand how they will be constructed.

What this home promises

Beyond bricks, stone and glass, a residence is really a promise about daily life. This one makes several.

  • Mornings on the balcony

    Tea and quiet, with the valley emerging from mist.

  • Evenings on the roof

    The sky turning gold, then indigo, beneath the pergola.

  • Bright, generous rooms

    Family life with views in almost every direction.

  • A garden through the seasons

    From spring blossom to autumn colour.

It also promises a sense of arrival. A home should feel special when you return to it, and the lantern-lit porch, the paved path and the warmly glowing windows all serve that purpose.

Conclusion

The upcoming residence of Mr. Mandyal at Purani Mandi is a thoughtful piece of hill architecture. Its two-volume composition gives it presence, its brick, stone and black steel palette gives it character, and its layered balconies and rooftop pergola give it a way of living that suits the landscape. The classical detailing lends dignity, and the generous glazing keeps the design bright, open and current.

Above all, the design shows respect: for the site, for the climate, for local architectural tradition and for the family who will live there. If the finished building matches the promise of these visualisations, it will be more than a handsome house. It will be a lasting home, one that sits comfortably among the hills of Himachal and grows in beauty with every season.
